# Interpret a deposit's engineering behaviour from its geological origin

> **Source:** `Soil Mechanics (Wiley Series in Geotechnical Engineering) by T. William Lambe Robert V. Whitman.docx` (Lambe & Whitman, Ch. 7 (Soil Formation), including residual soils, sedimentary deposits, fill, and §7.5 alterations of soil after formation), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** A ground model in which each stratum's likely fabric, variability, strength, compressibility and drainage behaviour is anticipated from how it was formed, guiding both the investigation and the design assumptions.

## Inputs

- Geological and soil survey mapping for the site and region
- Borehole logs describing colour, fabric, structure, bedding and inclusions, not just classification
- Site topography, landform and evidence of past processes (terraces, scarps, colluvial aprons, made ground)
- Groundwater and climate history, including any known dewatering, erosion or loading history

## Procedure

1. Classify each stratum by origin: residual (weathered in place from the parent rock), transported and sedimentary (alluvial, marine, lacustrine, glacial, aeolian), organic, or man-made fill.
2. For residual soils, expect strong vertical variability with depth of weathering, relict structure inherited from the parent rock (joints, bedding, quartz veins) that controls strength and permeability, and behaviour that is not captured by index tests alone.
3. For sedimentary deposits, expect layering, sorting and anisotropy set by the depositional environment; the source contrasts the flocculated fabric of a salt-water deposit with the more oriented fabric of a fresh-water deposit and with fully remoulded soil, and notes the large behavioural differences that follow.
4. For fills, treat the deposit as a sedimentary one in which every formation process was carried out by people, and establish how it was won, transported, placed and whether it was compacted, since those choices govern its density, uniformity and settlement behaviour.
5. Then consider post-formation alteration: assess how the deposit's properties have changed since formation through consolidation, erosion of overburden, desiccation, groundwater change, leaching, cementation, weathering, and how they will change further over the structure's design life.
6. Turn the interpretation into design consequences: expected variability and therefore borehole spacing; likely sampling difficulty; whether index correlations will work; whether relict structure requires larger-scale testing; whether strength will change over the design life.
7. Record the interpretation as an explicit part of the ground model so that later designers can test it against new information.

## Decision rules

- Do not apply correlations developed for transported sedimentary soils to residual soils without checking; relict structure makes index-property correlations unreliable there.
- Treat marine and estuarine clays as potentially sensitive and check sensitivity and salinity/leaching history before relying on undisturbed strength.
- Treat any deposit whose properties will change over the design life (softening on stress relief, swelling on wetting, further weathering, oxidation of sulfides) as requiring a design that covers both the initial and the changed condition.
- Uncontrolled fill of unknown provenance should be treated as unpredictable until proved otherwise, regardless of how well it classifies.

## Checks

- Check the interpreted origin against the regional geology and against the fabric actually logged in the cores.
- Check that the sampling and testing programme suits the origin (block samples or in-situ testing for structured residual soils, careful tube sampling for sensitive marine clays).
- Check whether the design-life changes identified have actually been carried into the design cases.

## Common errors

- Classifying a residual soil to a soil classification system and then applying transported-soil correlations to it.
- Assuming today's properties will persist for the design life.
- Treating fill as a single uniform stratum without establishing its placement history.

## Caveats

- Textbook learning material, not a design standard — verify method currency and all values against the source and Australian practice before use
- Lambe and Whitman was published in 1969; correlations, terminology and equipment described in it have been superseded in places — check current practice before using any value.
