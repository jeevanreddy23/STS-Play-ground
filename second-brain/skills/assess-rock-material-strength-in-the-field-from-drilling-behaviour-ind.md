# Assess rock material strength in the field from drilling behaviour, index tests and weathering grade

> **Source:** `Handbook of Geotechnical Investigation and Design Tables_Bert Look 2014.pdf` (Chapter 6 Rock strength parameters from classification and testing, Sections 6.1-6.17 (Tables 6.1 to 6.17), pp 73-84), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** A bracketed intact rock strength and a preliminary allowable bearing pressure derived from what the rig did, what the field tests gave and what the rock is - with the limits of each route stated.

## Inputs

- Drilling record: rig size, bit type (steel, tungsten carbide, rock roller), refusal depths, penetration rates
- SPT refusal records including seating and test drive blow counts
- Point load index results and Schmidt hammer rebound readings, where available
- Field index observations (scratch, knife, hammer response)
- RQD, weathering grade and rock type from the core log

## Procedure

1. Fix the strength definition being used before quoting a number - Table 6.1 lists the alternatives and Look notes that the value depends on confinement and failure mode.
2. Read strength from what the rig could do. Table 6.2 gives typical refusal levels for common rigs and bits in south east Queensland with the associated material and inferred SPT value; Table 6.3 converts drilling rig behaviour into indicative rock parameters. Insist the drilling supervisor cycles through bit types so refusal is a material property rather than a bit limitation.
3. Add field index evaluations from Table 6.4, which sets out the practical field methods for assessing intact rock strength and their interpretation.
4. Where point load testing is available, convert to strength using Table 6.5, and where a Schmidt hammer is available use Table 6.6 (N-type hammer) - both are screening tools, not substitutes for unconfined compression testing.
5. Use RQD as a first-approximation route to allowable bearing capacity via Table 6.7, but note Look's own qualification that RQD-based bearing pressures are a preliminary tool.
6. Bring weathering in with Tables 6.8 and 6.9. Table 6.8 gives the relative change in strength across weathering grades; Table 6.9 links field weathering evaluation to indicative parameters. Look warns that weathering alone cannot define a bearing capacity and that a more weathered rock of one type can be stronger than a less weathered rock of another.
7. Bracket against rock type using the three independent compilations in Tables 6.11, 6.12 and 6.13 (slope stability practice, field geology practice and engineering geology practice), then use Table 6.14, which combines and averages them, and Table 6.15, which converts combined strength and defect considerations into an estimate of allowable bearing capacity in rock. The spread between the three sources is the honest uncertainty band.
8. Screen durability with Table 6.16 before exposing or reusing the material, and check Table 6.17 for how the rock will behave once excavated and placed as fill - some rocks logged as rock behave as soil in earthworks.
9. Protect the founding surface. Where weathered shales, siltstones or sandstones are exposed, specify a blinding layer or other protection because deterioration on exposure is rapid.
10. Present the result as a range with the derivation routes listed, and state which route governs and why.

## Decision rules

- Do not set a design bearing pressure from weathering grade alone; combine it with rock type, strength and defect spacing.
- Rig refusal is a function of rig weight and bit as much as of rock - state the equipment when quoting a refusal-based strength.
- For low strength rocks, check that the calculated capacity is physically sensible - Look flags that some published methods return unrealistically low capacities in weak rock.
- The allowable bearing capacity changes roughly by an order of magnitude across the rock classification range in Table 6.10, so getting the class right matters more than refining within a class.
- Non-durable rock can lose bearing capacity after exposure - the design value must be the post-exposure value unless the surface is protected.

## Checks

- Strength estimate from at least two of: drilling behaviour, field index, point load, Schmidt hammer, RQD, rock type table
- Weathering grade consistent with the strength estimate and with adjacent holes
- Durability screening completed before founding on or reusing sedimentary or foliated rock
- Unconfined compression or point load testing scheduled where the rock strength governs
- Bearing pressures cross-checked against Chapter 22 rock foundation methods before issue

## Common errors

- Quoting an allowable bearing pressure from weathering grade in a preliminary report and having it adopted as the design value
- Treating a light rig's refusal as bedrock
- Using point load or Schmidt hammer correlations as design strengths without laboratory confirmation
- Ignoring durability, then finding the founding surface has softened before concrete is placed
- Averaging the three rock strength compilations into a single number and losing the uncertainty band

## Caveats

- Reference text, not a design standard - verify against the source pages and Australian practice before use.
- Refusal-level tables are specific to south east Queensland materials and to the rigs of that market.
- Presumed bearing pressures from field description are preliminary values; Look's Preface names their uncritical carry-through into detailed design as a recurring failure.
