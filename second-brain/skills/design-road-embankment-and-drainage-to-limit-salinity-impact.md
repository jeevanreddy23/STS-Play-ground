# Design road embankment and drainage to limit salinity impact

> **Source:** `roads-and-salinity.pdf` (Basic Road Construction, including good road building materials, construction of layers, stabilisation or modification of layers (pp. 3-5); The Impact of Roads on Salinity — road design in flat terrain diagram (Options A and B) and groundwater issues in hilly terrain diagram (pp. 13-14); Strategies to Prevent or Minimise Salinity Damage, with the 'Bringing Extra Salt to the Site' case study and its preventative measures and recommended treatment table (p. 18). DIPNR, 2003.), held in the local reference library — see

> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** A road cross-section, drainage layout and materials specification chosen so the road neither concentrates recharge nor draws salt into the pavement, with the embankment-versus-deep-drain trade-off made explicitly.

## Inputs

- Terrain setting: flat or hilly, and where the alignment sits relative to the zone of lateral groundwater movement
- Depth to water table and its seasonal range
- Salt content of proposed pavement and subgrade materials, by dry mass of aggregate
- Salt content of construction water (bore water, recycled water, any saline source)
- Fines content of the proposed pavement material
- Discharge points available for surface runoff and for subsoil drainage, kept separate
- Locations of proposed sedimentation and detention basins
- Location of saline discharge areas along the route

## Procedure

1. In flat terrain, choose deliberately between the two options rather than defaulting. Option A is a low embankment with deep, high-volume drains intended to intercept and lower the water table. Option B is a higher, or stabilised, embankment with lower-volume drains and subsoil drains only where needed.
2. Weigh Option A's drawbacks explicitly: the deep drains become direct entry points for surface water into the water table and increase recharge, the intercepted saline water has to be disposed of, and if the table rises the drains and disposal system can overflow and spread saline water into the surrounding landscape.
3. Weigh Option B's cost and safety consequences: a higher embankment carries the extra earthworks cost and a road safety penalty, against a longer life for the strengthened structure and a clean separation of surface drainage from subsoil drainage so each can be discharged separately.
4. In hilly terrain, identify the more permeable weathered colluvium overlying less permeable rock, and site the road so cut and compaction do not sever the lateral groundwater path through that zone. Cutting into or compacting it retards transmission, builds salinity in the groundwater behind the road and produces saline runoff and poor soil health downslope.
5. Design drainage so it does not excessively concentrate surface runoff into one place, which causes waterlogging of the pavement or a local recharge point.
6. Improve shoulder cross-falls and table drains to reduce ponding, and increase seal width by including the shoulders in the sealing programme — this supports the outer pavement edge as well as managing surface water.
7. Route surface runoff away from saline discharge areas, which are more susceptible to erosion and to mobilising high salt concentrations.
8. Specify materials and construction water with minimal salt content, and design surface characteristics — compaction, stabilisation, sealing — to limit surface permeability so saline water is not drawn up through the pavement layers by evaporation.
9. Sequence construction so the seal goes on as soon as possible after the pavement is compacted, and traffic or roll the section immediately.
10. Site sedimentation and detention basins with care, since basins leak and a concentration of salts in the leakage can degrade soil structure and increase the leakage further.

## Decision rules

- Where salt-affected material is a risk, limit soluble salts in pavement material to a low ceiling by dry mass of aggregate (the referenced highway investigation used a 0.25 per cent limit, applied to at least 0.5 m depth wherever possible) — the failure case that prompted it had material exceeding 10 per cent soluble salts.
- High fines content makes a material more susceptible to salt build-up and crystallisation and worsens the failure even at a given salt content.
- The failure mechanism to design against is salt crystallisation under the prime or primer seal, which raises vapour pressure and blisters and heaves the seal.
- Separate surface drainage from subsoil drainage so the subsoil (potentially saline, low volume) can be discharged independently of surface runoff.
- Deep drains that intercept the water table create a disposal liability and a recharge pathway; do not adopt them without a disposal plan.
- Salinity management starts with good general practice and a site assessment for salinity before design and construction, not with a treatment after failure.

## Checks

- Confirm soluble salt content of both aggregate and construction water was tested, not assumed, and that the sampling covered the full depth of the specified layer.
- Confirm the drainage design has an actual discharge point for any intercepted saline water, and that discharge has approval.
- Confirm subsoil and surface drainage systems are not connected.
- Confirm the shoulder and table drain cross-falls remove ponding rather than moving it.
- Confirm basin siting has been checked against soil structure sensitivity to leaked saline water.
- Confirm the construction programme actually allows sealing immediately after compaction.

## Common errors

- Adopting deep drains as a water table control without accounting for the recharge pathway they create or where the salt water goes.
- Cutting through the permeable colluvium in hilly terrain and turning the road into a groundwater dam.
- Using saline bore water or sea water for compaction because it is the water available.
- Specifying a salt limit on the base material only, and not through the full depth or to the construction water.
- Leaving a compacted pavement unsealed and untrafficked long enough for salt to crystallise under the prime.
- Concentrating road drainage into a single outfall that waterlogs the pavement or creates a recharge hotspot.

## Caveats

- 2003 publication citing a South Australian highway investigation from the early 1980s and a NSW RTA interim guideline from 2002. Treat the salt limits and seal treatment rates as historical values to verify against current pavement specifications before use.
- Numeric limits above are cited from the referenced case study only; confirm against the current controlled specification.
- Complements rather than replaces the existing skills on pavement rehabilitation in saline areas, surface treatment selection by salt content, and lime stabilisation of saline subgrades — this one covers the design-stage siting, cross-section and drainage decisions those do not.
