# Stabilise road subgrades with lime in saline and wet conditions

> **Source:** `Techniques-to-use-on-roads-affected-by-salinity.pdf` (Sections 5.3.2 (Table 3) and 5.5, pp. 10 and 13-14), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** A lime-stabilised subgrade that is drier, stiffer, less moisture-reactive and lower-permeability, reducing water and salt movement into the pavement and watertable.

## Inputs

- Subgrade characterisation: clay content, PI, CBR (soaked/unsoaked), organic matter, seasonal moisture behaviour
- Binder choice: quicklime or hydrated lime (or cementitious/polymer alternatives)
- Mixing plant capable of at least two-pass mixing

## Procedure

1. Use lime stabilisation of clay subgrades to: increase subgrade stiffness, reduce PI, improve volumetric stability, stiffen subbase layers, and create a temporary construction platform.
2. Understand the two-stage mechanism: calcium cation exchange first agglomerates clay fines and 'dewaters' the clay; then at pH above about 12, pozzolanic reactions form calcium silicates/aluminates that gel, bind and slowly crystallise — strength gain is temperature-dependent and can continue for months (trackable by CBR).
3. Mix thoroughly with a minimum of two mixing passes per stabilisation best practice; allow reaction time before trafficking.
4. Rely on the permeability benefit: strongly modified clay shows decreased permeability (void occlusion by reaction products, per Stocker's ARRB work), so less water enters, less salt diffuses in, and subgrade strength loss is diminished.
5. Use lime to bind seasonally shrink-swell subgrades so cracks do not propagate into pavement or footpaths and admit more water.
6. For very weak subgrades (CBR under 3% at construction), the common convention is to ignore the structural gain of light lime/cement stabilisation and design at CBR 3%, unless using explicit stabilised-subgrade design procedures.

## Decision rules

- Choose modification vs bound behaviour deliberately (indicative Austroads bands: modified 0.7-1.5 MPa UCS; lightly bound 1.5-3.0 MPa; bound above 3.0 MPa) — bound layers cut water entry but crack and must have cracks sealed (crack sealing, SAMI or crumb rubber seals).
- Organic matter in the soil, even in small quantities, can reduce the lime reaction's effectiveness — test and adjust.
- No evidence is cited that salt degrades lime-stabilised material, but the paper notes the salt effect on cementitious/bituminous stabilised strength was unquantified — treat high-salt cases with trials rather than assumption.

## Checks

- Lime spread rate and mixing uniformity verified (two-pass minimum).
- Strength gain tracked over time with CBR/UCS testing rather than assumed at 7 days.
- Shrinkage cracks in bound layers sealed promptly after appearing.

## Common errors

- Single-pass mixing leaving unreacted clay pockets.
- Expecting full strength quickly in cold weather (crystallisation is temperature-dependent).
- Treating lime stabilisation as a substitute for subsurface drainage instead of a complement.
- Ignoring organics that quench the pozzolanic reaction.

## Caveats

- Classification and aggressivity calls must be verified against the source document, current Austroads stabilisation guides and the project spec — the UCS class bands cited were from a draft guide.
- The absence of evidence for salt degrading lime-stabilised layers is not evidence of absence; the author explicitly calls for Australian research on strength loss at various salt levels.
- Wyndham City standard configurations (lime-stabilised clay subgrades under granular/asphalt pavements by traffic tier) are context for expansive, not necessarily saline, subgrades.

## Standards cited
