# Estimate axial pile capacity and check pile group and negative skin friction effects

> **Source:** `Soil Mechanics (Wiley Series in Geotechnical Engineering) by T. William Lambe Robert V. Whitman.docx` (Lambe & Whitman, Ch. 33 (Deep Foundations), including the static capacity expressions for free-draining and non-free-draining soil, worked capacity examples, pile driving and dynamic formulas, negative skin friction, and movements caused by pile driving), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** A design axial capacity for a single pile from static analysis, cross-checked against driving observations and load testing, with group effects, settlement and downdrag assessed.

## Inputs

- Ground profile with strength parameters: effective friction angle and effective vertical stress for free-draining soils, undrained strength for non-free-draining soils
- Pile type, material, cross-sectional area, perimeter and embedded length
- Whether the pile is driven or bored, and the construction effects that follow
- Group geometry: number of piles, spacing, cap arrangement
- Any fill placed over soft ground, present or future, and any planned dewatering nearby

## Procedure

1. Compute the static capacity as the sum of end bearing and shaft resistance.
2. For a free-draining soil, take end bearing as the pile base area times the effective vertical stress at the base times a bearing capacity factor, and shaft resistance as the sum over the shaft of perimeter area times the effective vertical stress times an earth pressure coefficient times the tangent of the interface friction angle. The source gives an indicative range for the earth pressure coefficient and notes the interface friction angle depends on pile material.
3. For a non-free-draining soil, take end bearing from the undrained strength times the undrained bearing capacity factor plus the total overburden, and shaft resistance from an adhesion related to the remoulded, reconsolidated undrained strength along the shaft.
4. Apply the appropriate factor of safety or geotechnical strength reduction factor; the source notes a global factor of about two is commonly used on computed pile capacity, reflecting the large uncertainty in static pile formulas.
5. Cross-check driven pile capacity against driving records. Dynamic formulas of the Engineering News type derive resistance from the hammer energy, an allowance for energy losses and the measured set per blow; treat them as crude and use them for consistency checking and for detecting anomalies between piles, not as the primary capacity determination.
6. Where the design is capacity-critical, verify with static load testing or high-strain dynamic testing on a proportion of the piles.
7. Check group behaviour: group capacity may be less than the sum of individual capacities at close spacing, and group settlement is governed by the stress bulb of the whole group, which extends far deeper than that of a single pile.
8. Check negative skin friction (downdrag). Where fill is placed over soft soil, or where the soil around the shaft settles for any reason, the soil drags down on the pile and adds load rather than resisting it. Only a small relative movement between soil and shaft is needed to mobilise it fully.
9. Assess the causes of surrounding-soil settlement the source identifies: fill over soft ground, disturbance and excess pore pressures generated by driving the pile itself, driving on adjacent sites, and dewatering even at considerable distance.
10. Assess construction effects on neighbours: driving causes heave and settlement of nearby ground and structures, and vibration.

## Decision rules

- Be very reluctant to drive piles through fill freshly placed over soft soil — the source warns this combination produces the most severe downdrag, and cases exist of piles being pulled away from the structures they support.
- Downdrag adds load; it must be added to the structural load on the pile, not subtracted from capacity, and the pile section must be checked for it.
- Static pile formulas are approximate; where the consequences of shortfall are serious, load test.
- Dynamic driving formulas do not account properly for soil setup or relaxation; re-strike after a delay before concluding a pile is short.
- Group settlement, not single-pile capacity, usually controls the design of a large pile group over a compressible deposit.

## Checks

- Check the computed static capacity against driving records and against any load test results, and reconcile differences before proceeding.
- Check whether any future filling, dewatering or adjacent construction could initiate downdrag over the design life.
- Check group settlement using the stress distribution from the whole group, not the single pile.

## Common errors

- Summing single-pile capacities for a closely spaced group without a group efficiency check.
- Relying on a driving formula alone for capacity.
- Ignoring downdrag where fill is to be placed around existing piles.

## Caveats

- Textbook learning material, not a design standard — verify method currency and all values against the source and Australian practice before use
- Lambe and Whitman was published in 1969; correlations, terminology and equipment described in it have been superseded in places — check current practice before using any value.
- Bearing capacity factors, earth pressure coefficient ranges and adhesion values are given in the source; Australian piling design follows AS 2159 with its own risk-based reduction factors.
