# Develop a fill, backfill, drainage and durability material specification

> **Source:** `Handbook of Geotechnical Investigation and Design Tables_Bert Look 2014.pdf` (Chapter 17 Fill specifications, Sections 17.1-17.13 (Tables 17.1 to 17.13), pp 249-257), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** Material specifications for pavement aggregate, structural backfill, drainage media, pipe bedding, earth linings, dam cores and rock armour that are written around the property the material must deliver, with a testing frequency that suits the lot size.

## Inputs

- Function of the material (structural fill, drainage, filter, bedding, lining, core, armour, pavement layer)
- Available site-won materials and their grading, plasticity and durability test results
- Design requirements: strength, permeability, filter compatibility, durability, erosion resistance
- Structure type it serves (reinforced soil wall, pipe, dam, revetment, breakwater, pavement)
- Lot sizes and production volumes for the testing regime

## Procedure

1. Start from the property required, not the material available. Table 17.1 lists desirable material properties by application, and Look's framing is that grain size is the usual proxy - more fines for low permeability and cohesion, fewer for drainage and strength - so write the specification around the governing property and use grading as the control.
2. For pavement materials use Table 17.2 to develop the specification: larger nominal size gives strength but excessive size causes segregation and workability problems, a well graded envelope gives optimum strength, and some fines are needed to close the envelope but excess fines destroy it. Natural river gravels typically carry more fines than crushed rock for the same nominal size. Add durability requirements from Table 17.8, with the wearing and base courses set higher than the sub-base.
3. For structural backfill behind reinforced soil structures use Table 17.3: cap the maximum particle size to protect the reinforcement, keep the material free of organic and deleterious matter, and note reinforced soil slopes tolerate more fines and larger sizes than walls.
4. For drainage media use Table 17.4 - uniformly graded, more permeable than the surrounding soil - and cross-check against the fines sensitivity in Table 8.8, where a small percentage of fine material can drop permeability by orders of magnitude. Define clean sand by a fines ceiling.
5. For pipe bedding use Table 17.5: a well graded envelope gives the best support, but a single size is often specified with reduced compaction because it is more robust to poor placement; larger sizes support larger pipes but are unsuitable for small ones. Specify minimum cover, higher under heavy construction traffic, and call out haunch compaction as the difficult and commonly failed item.
6. For low permeability linings use Table 17.6: a clay fraction above a stated threshold, a specified density relative to standard maximum dry density, and a control test frequency per volume placed. For dam cores use Table 17.9 to classify core material for resistance to internal erosion, with a minimum clay content and a warning that very high plasticity clays are hard to place and compact, and that stones reduce erosion potential up to a point beyond which they create seepage paths.
7. For rock armour, revetments and breakwaters use Table 17.11 (armour, rip rap, stone pitching), Table 17.12 (durability indicators) and Table 17.13 (durability by armour function, primary higher than secondary). Look's rule is that several durability indicators should point the same way before a rock is classified as durable, and that strong durable rock can be placed in thick lifts while weak but durable rock needs thin lifts.
8. For placement on slopes use Table 17.7 - compaction is systematically poorer at edges and sloping faces, moisture near the plastic limit is needed for proper compaction, and the achievable compacted layer thickness scales with roller weight. Overbuild and trim rather than compacting to the final face.
9. Set testing frequency from Table 17.10 by area, project size and material uniformity. Where a lot has fewer than the minimum sample count for a statistical scheme, specify a no-value-shall-be-less-than criterion instead of a characteristic value.
10. Cross-reference the compaction requirements (Tables 17.14 to 17.19) so the material specification and the compaction specification are consistent - particularly on standard versus modified reference and on moisture at placement.

## Decision rules

- Import better quality fill where consistency matters more than unit cost - Look notes an imported material can be more economical overall than processing a variable site-won material, but also that the selected material zone exists to use the best job-won material first.
- Do not specify a drainage material by grading alone; control fines explicitly.
- Durability classification requires multiple concordant indicators, not a single test.
- Lift thickness for rock fill follows durability class - weak rock needs thin lifts irrespective of its strength when fresh.
- Below the statistical minimum sample count, conformity is judged on minimum values, not on characteristic values.

## Checks

- Grading envelope achievable by the nominated source and stable through handling
- Fines content controlled where permeability or filter function is required
- Filter compatibility checked against the soil being filtered (Chapter 15 Table 15.30)
- Durability tests scheduled for sedimentary and foliated materials before acceptance
- Testing frequency matched to lot size and material variability per Table 17.10

## Common errors

- Writing a grading specification that no local source can consistently meet
- Allowing fines into a drainage layer and losing the drainage function
- Accepting rock armour on a single durability test
- Specifying compaction of pipe bedding without addressing haunch compaction
- Applying statistical conformity to lots with too few samples

## Caveats

- Reference text, not a design standard - verify against the source pages and Australian practice before use.
- Specification content must be reconciled with the project's contract specification, which governs.
- Sources cited are international; Australian road authority specifications differ in test methods and limits.
