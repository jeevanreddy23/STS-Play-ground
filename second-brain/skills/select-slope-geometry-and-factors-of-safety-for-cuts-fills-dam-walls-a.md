# Select slope geometry and factors of safety for cuts, fills, dam walls and channels

> **Source:** `Handbook of Geotechnical Investigation and Design Tables_Bert Look 2014.pdf` (Chapter 14 Slopes, Sections 14.1-14.31 (Tables 14.1 to 14.31), pp 199-218), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** Batter angles and target factors of safety chosen against the consequence of failure, the quality of the data and the analysis method, with the equivalent probability of failure stated alongside.

## Inputs

- Slope height, material type and strength parameters with their variability
- Groundwater regime including extreme and drawdown conditions
- Whether the slope is new or existing, permanent or temporary
- Consequence of failure: risk to life, economic and environmental exposure
- Analysis method and whether moment or force equilibrium governs
- For dams and channels: retained height, drawdown rate, zoning, lining

## Procedure

1. Express the geometry consistently. Table 14.1 sets the slope descriptors and the vertical-to-horizontal convention; note soil slopes rarely exceed the very steep band without reinforcement while rock slopes can be sub-vertical.
2. Identify the failure mechanism before selecting a factor of safety. Table 14.2 lists macro factors causing slope movement and Table 14.3 the micro-scale causes, grouped as either a decrease in strength or an increase in stress. Water is implicated in most of them.
3. Understand what the factor of safety depends on before choosing a target. Table 14.4 lists the dependencies - which strength percentile was used, height, slope angle, benching, stratification and dip of weakness planes, unit weight and whether buoyant weight applies, surcharge, water conditions, and the analysis method (method of slices versus wedge methods give different answers on identical input).
4. Select the target using Table 14.5, which sets out equivalent minimum factors of safety for different input assumptions so that a lower factor computed on conservative inputs can be recognised as equivalent to a higher factor computed on best-estimate inputs. Use it to prevent double conservatism.
5. Distinguish new from existing slopes: Table 14.7 for new slopes and Table 14.8 for existing, with the new-slope target higher to allow for future changes in load, strength and water conditions that an existing slope has already experienced.
6. Set the target against consequence using Table 14.9 (risk to life, considering both number of people exposed and duration of exposure) and Table 14.10 (economic and environmental risk, which Look extends to political and reputational consequence).
7. State the equivalent probability of failure. Use Table 14.6 to convert factor of safety to probability of failure, and cross-check against Chapter 10 Tables 10.18, 10.24 and 10.25. Reporting both numbers makes the risk legible to non-geotechnical decision makers.
8. For preliminary geometry, enter Table 14.11 for typical excavated batters by material and Table 14.12 for fill batters, remembering both assume no surcharge at the crest and that the underlying material strength often governs fill slope stability. Treat both as a guide requiring a stability analysis.
9. For dam walls, use Table 14.13 for factors of safety, Tables 14.14 and 14.15 for typical slopes of low homogeneous walls and the effect of height, and Table 14.16 for design elements. Zoned embankments allow steeper slopes and are recommended above a stated height, but require a full stability analysis. Note the low drawdown rates that can still count as rapid.
10. For water-retaining and conveyance structures use Table 14.17 (levees and canals), Tables 14.18 to 14.20 (revetment slopes and crest levels), Table 14.21 (underwater slopes) and Table 14.22 (canal side slopes by material).
11. For seismic conditions use Table 14.23, and read the displacement criterion in Chapter 23 - where the pseudo-static factor of safety falls below the stated value, the assessment moves from factor of safety to deformation.
12. For rock slopes use Tables 14.25 and 14.26 for design and stability factors, Tables 14.27 to 14.28 for rockfall and coefficients of restitution, Table 14.29 for stabilisation measures and Table 14.30 for rock trap ditch geometry - and cross-check against the project's existing rock slope skills.
13. For temporary trenching apply Table 14.31: shoring is required beyond a shallow depth, an excavation becomes an open cutting once its width greatly exceeds its depth, and stockpile and plant standoff distances must be set to protect the trench.
14. Add surface protection: Table 14.24 for stable topsoil slopes and Chapter 15 for erosion control on the completed batter.

## Decision rules

- Do not compare factors of safety produced under different input assumptions without first mapping them through Table 14.5.
- New slopes carry a higher target factor of safety than existing slopes of the same geometry.
- Temporary works may use reduced factors of safety; critical areas and projects use higher ones - state which applies and why.
- Where the seismic pseudo-static factor of safety approaches unity, switch to a displacement-based assessment.
- Batter tables are preliminary only; a stability analysis is required for anything other than low slopes in known ground.
- Report the factor of safety with the probability of failure - the number alone carries no information about consequence.

## Checks

- Water condition analysed for both the most likely case and the extreme case
- Buoyant unit weight applied below the phreatic surface in the stability model
- Method of analysis stated and its effect on the computed factor of safety acknowledged
- Surcharge at the crest included where plant, stockpiles or structures are possible
- Factor of safety target justified against consequence tables 14.9 and 14.10
- Trial surfaces include non-circular and structurally controlled mechanisms where stratification or defects exist

## Common errors

- Using batter tables as a design without any stability analysis
- Applying an existing-slope factor of safety to a new cut
- Double conservatism - lower-bound strengths combined with a high factor of safety target
- Ignoring rapid drawdown because the drawdown rate seems slow
- Omitting crest surcharge from construction plant and stockpiles
- Quoting a factor of safety without stating the water condition it applies to

## Caveats

- Reference text, not a design standard - verify against the source pages and Australian practice before use.
- Dam guidance is limited to low walls; medium and high dams require detailed design under dam-specific guidelines.
- Trenching guidance does not substitute for the applicable work health and safety regulation.
