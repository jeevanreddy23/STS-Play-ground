# Audit site investigation quality, sampling adequacy and specimen size before accepting the data

> **Source:** `Handbook of Geotechnical Investigation and Design Tables_Bert Look 2014.pdf` (Chapter 1, Sections 1.12 and 1.14-1.20 (Tables 1.12, 1.14, 1.15, 1.16, 1.17, 1.19, 1.20), pp 11-17; Preface pp xxi-xxii), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** A written judgement on whether an investigation is good, normal or poor quality, with the specific gaps identified, so design either proceeds on the data or the data is supplemented before design.

## Inputs

- Factual report: number and depth of holes, tests per hectare, sampling record, laboratory schedule
- Site area and development type
- Soil fabric indications (fissuring, sand partings, sensitivity, pedal structure) from the logs
- Tests intended to be run on the samples and the stress range of interest
- Investigation budget as a percentage of capital works

## Procedure

1. Score the investigation against Look Table 1.17. The table lists influencing factors - phasing, safety and environmental plan, tests per hectare (separate thresholds for buildings/bridges versus roads), extent and depth appropriate to the development, sample amount, specimen size accounting for structure, percentage of samples laboratory tested, samples tested at the relevant stress range, and budget as a percentage of capital works. Count how many factors are met and read the good / fair / poor banding.
2. Check sample quantity against the intended tests. Enter Table 1.14 with the test type (stabilisation, CBR, compaction curve, particle size by top size, hydrometer, Atterberg) to read the minimum disturbed mass required - insufficient mass is a common reason a laboratory schedule silently shrinks.
3. Check specimen diameter against soil fabric using Table 1.16 (Rowe). Enter with clay type and macro-fabric - non-fissured, pedal or with silt/sand layers, fissured with plain or silt-filled fissures, jointed, pre-existing slip - and the parameter wanted (undrained strength, effective strength, coefficient of volume compressibility, coefficient of consolidation), and read the required specimen size. Fissured and jointed fabrics demand much larger specimens for strength than for compressibility.
4. Account for sampling disturbance when reading strengths. Table 1.15 shows the direction and magnitude of the change in undrained shear strength with material type and plasticity - soft low-plasticity clays lose strength badly, stiff high-plasticity clays can read artificially high. Adjust expectations before comparing laboratory strength with in-situ tests.
5. Check the volume-sampled reality check in Table 1.12: for typical developments the sampled volume is a vanishingly small fraction of the loaded volume. Use this explicitly when defending the size of the extrapolation the design rests on.
6. Check the laboratory/field split of spend. Look notes a typical split of investigation cost between field and laboratory work and flags the trend to cut laboratory testing in favour of inferred properties as a commercial rather than technical decision; call that out where it has happened.
7. Where the audit lands in fair or poor, list which specific Table 1.17 factors failed and specify supplementary work to close them, rather than applying a blanket conservatism factor.

## Decision rules

- A good investigation does not need every factor in Table 1.17 - Look's benchmark is that a majority of the influencing factors are present, weighted by project relevance.
- Where the intended parameter is undrained strength in a fissured or jointed clay, small-diameter tube specimens are not fit for purpose regardless of how many were taken.
- Do not re-use a sample across tests that alter it (for example, a further compaction on material already compacted) - degradation invalidates the second result.
- Sample and test at least every 1.5 m and at every strata change; undisturbed sampling in clays, penetration testing in granular material.
- The quality of an investigation is primarily set by driller and supervising engineer experience and plant adequacy - factors that a cost comparison does not reveal. Table 1.17 covers only the secondary, auditable factors.

## Checks

- Tests per hectare against the building/bridge and road thresholds in Table 1.17
- Percentage of recovered samples actually laboratory tested against the banding in Table 1.17
- Specimen sizes used versus Table 1.16 for the observed fabric
- Sample masses received versus Table 1.14 minimums for each scheduled test
- Whether samples were tested at the in-situ and post-construction stress range, not a default cell pressure

## Common errors

- Judging an investigation by borehole count alone without checking depth, sampling and laboratory follow-through
- Testing at a convenient stress rather than the relevant overburden plus loading range
- Ignoring fabric when selecting specimen diameter, then treating the low result as the design value
- Treating a low laboratory strength in a disturbed soft clay as conservative when the sampling method caused it
- Reporting good coverage without acknowledging the sampled-to-loaded volume ratio

## Caveats

- Reference text, not a design standard - verify against the source pages and Australian practice before use.
- Table 1.17 applies equal weight to unequal factors; Look states the weighting is project specific.
- Table 1.16 (Rowe 1972) predates modern sampling equipment; treat the sizes as minimum guidance rather than an upper bound.
