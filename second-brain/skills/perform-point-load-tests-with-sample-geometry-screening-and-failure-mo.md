# Perform point load tests with sample-geometry screening and failure-mode vetting

> **Source:** [YouTube — Point Load Test: How to Break Rock in One Minute — Experimental procedure](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=miKpYQ-VeUs&t=1s) (00:01-10:16). A YouTube lecture — learning
> material, not an approved design procedure. Equations and numbers were
> heard through auto-captions; read them off the video before use, and
> verify anything design-relevant against the standard and the project
> specification. Engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** Valid peak failure loads from point load tests on rock core, with unsuitable samples and invalid failure modes screened out

## Inputs

- Rock core samples or lump/disk samples of the same rock type (at least three, preferably four or five)
- Point load apparatus with force readout
- Calipers or rule for measuring diameter, length, width and thickness
- Safety glasses

## Procedure

1. For a diametral test on core: measure diameter D and length L, and confirm L is greater than half the diameter before accepting the sample (D about 51 mm and L about 32 mm passed in the demo)
2. For an axial/disk test: measure thickness D and width W and check D against the standard's geometric limits relative to W (a 20 mm thick disk on a 50 mm core failed the check and was rejected)
3. Wear safety glasses, seat the sample between the two conical platens
4. Increase the load slowly until the sample breaks and record the peak force in kN (3.2 kN for the first sandstone core)
5. Record the measured dimensions alongside the peak force in the test spreadsheet
6. Inspect the failure surface after every break: the failure must pass through the sample between the platens, not spall off a side or corner
7. Repeat on further samples of the same rock type until at least three valid results exist

## Decision rules

- Reject a test where failure ran along a pre-existing crack or broke only at the sample edge (seen with a weathered sample that failed at 0.265 kN); weathered samples are prone to this
- One or two samples is never enough — a minimum of three tests per rock type, more if material is available
- Re-measure the sample thickness before a retest — the relevant dimension changes after a partial break (39.4 mm on the re-test in the demo)

## Checks

- Geometry check passed and recorded before loading
- Failure plane inspected and judged valid before the force reading is kept
- Peak force and dimensions written down immediately per test

## Common errors

- Writing down every peak force without looking at how the sample actually failed
- Testing disks that are too thin relative to their width
- Drawing conclusions from a single test on a variable, weathered rock

## Caveats

- YouTube lecture — learning material, not an approved design procedure; verify against project specification and current standards
- The lecturer refers to 'the standard' for sample geometry limits without naming it; the axial-test thickness-to-width limits are garbled in auto-captions — equation shown on screen — read from the video
- Low anomalous results from weathered samples may still be recorded for information but should be flagged, not pooled blindly with valid results
