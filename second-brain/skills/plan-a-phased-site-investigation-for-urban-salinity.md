# Plan a phased site investigation for urban salinity

> **Source:** `booklet3siteinvestigationsforurbansalinity (2).pdf` (Whole booklet, esp. pp. 3-7 (phases, Table 1) and pp. 15-17 (Phase 4)), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** A staged investigation (desktop review through to management plan) scaled to the development type, that identifies salt stores, water movement and the two-way impacts between the development and salinity processes.

## Inputs

- Proposed development type, layout and mapping scale (1:25 000 down to 1:1 000)
- Existing information: geological maps, soil landscape maps, urban capability maps, climate data, land use history, SALIS soil profiles, groundwater bore database records
- Initial site walk observations (salinity indicators, field EC readings of dams/creeks)
- Budget context (cost of testing vs value of the asset)

## Procedure

1. Phase 1: run a desktop review of the site and vicinity (geology, soils, groundwater, climate, land use history) and walk the site recording indicators such as bare scalds, surface salt crystals, puffy or black-stained soil, indicator vegetation, tree dieback and stained foundations; take field EC1:5 screening readings where salinity is suspected.
2. Map geomorphic landform units (crests, side slopes, foot slopes) since landform controls where salt outbreaks and accumulations are likely; note structural controls such as dykes and rock bars.
3. Phase 2: design and execute a detailed investigation — describe soil profiles per landform unit (arranged as transects for a 3D picture), dig test pits/backhoe pits, install piezometers where groundwater understanding is missing.
4. Phase 3: laboratory-analyse selected representative profiles and present results against standards and reference documents.
5. Phase 4: interpret current and likely future conditions, address cumulative impacts, and select management options (e.g. minimise infiltration, retain deep-rooted vegetation, salt-tolerant landscaping, drainage, material selection), then set up monitoring (retain piezometers through and after construction).

## Decision rules

- Set sample numbers from the mapping scale and land use intensity using the booklet's Table 1 (p. 6): denser sampling for larger-scale mapping and more intensive uses (e.g. roughly 6-18 initial observations per km2 at 1:25 000 open space, up to 50-100 per ha at 1:1 000 highly intensive construction).
- Use fewer samples when landscape/soil variability is low, local knowledge is good, or the land use will not mobilise salt (no irrigation, effluent disposal or clearing); use more when the opposite holds.
- Weigh sampling cost against asset value (the booklet's example: minimal testing for a small shed vs testing to justify sulphate-resistant cement for a house).
- Consider EMI (electromagnetic induction) survey with a few validation soil tests as an alternative to dense grid sampling.
- If Phase 1 shows little salt/groundwater hazard or well-understood processes, reduce Phase 2 scope.

## Checks

- At least one soil profile per landform unit, with profiles arranged in transects across the site.
- Field observations reconcile with lab results (a mapped salt scald should return high lab salinity; if not, suspect labelling or reporting errors).
- Groundwater interpretation distinguishes local, intermediate and regional systems and recharge vs discharge behaviour before management options are chosen.
- Piezometers are located where they can survive construction for ongoing monitoring.

## Common errors

- Treating a single water-body EC reading (ECw) as representative of soil salinity — surface water EC varies enormously in the short term and cannot be used to infer soil salinity.
- Assuming low soil-salinity results mean no future problem: small salt stores can be mobilised and concentrated by the development, and saline groundwater can rise from off-site causes.
- Applying one prescriptive test list to every site instead of tailoring the program to site processes.
- Ignoring cumulative impacts of many small developments.

## Caveats

- Classification and aggressivity calls must be verified against the source document and the project specification before use.
- The booklet dates from 2002 (costs, agency names and AS 2159-1995 references are dated; the appendix itself redirects to AS 2159-2009, now further superseded).
- Sampling densities in Table 1 are recommendations, not statutory minimums; council/consent authority requirements govern.
- Complementary context in booklet5new.pdf (Introduction to Urban Salinity) and INDUSTRY_GUIDE_T56_Residential_Slabs_and_Footings_in_Saline_Environments.pdf Section 3.
