# Screen a tunnel for squeezing using the rock-mass-strength to in-situ-stress ratio

> **Source:** [YouTube — Tunnel Squeezing: Predicting and Estimating Ground Deformation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hVgGXjpDYXg&t=0s) (00:00-04:57). A YouTube lecture — learning
> material, not an approved design procedure. Equations and numbers were
> heard through auto-captions; read them off the video before use, and
> verify anything design-relevant against the standard and the project
> specification. Engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** An estimate of expected tunnel strain and squeezing severity class for a tunnel in weak rock, from an empirical strength/stress chart

## Inputs

- Rock mass strength (given or separately estimated; low values typify shale, mudstone, sheared flysch)
- Tunnel depth below surface
- Rock unit weight (assume an average of 27 kN/m3, i.e. 0.027 MPa per metre, when unmeasured)
- Empirical chart relating strength/stress ratio to tunnel strain and squeezing severity

## Procedure

1. Compute the in-situ vertical stress as unit weight times depth; with the average-rock assumption use 0.027 times depth in metres to get MPa (example: 44 m depth gives about 1.19 MPa)
2. Form the ratio of rock mass strength to in-situ stress (example: 0.17 / 1.19 = 0.14)
3. Enter the empirical chart at that ratio and read the expected strain percentage (example: slightly above 5 percent)
4. Classify the squeezing severity from the strain band the reading falls in (strain between about 5 and 10 percent indicated very severe squeezing problems in the example)

## Decision rules

- Low strength/stress ratios mean high strain: the lower the ratio, the more severe the squeezing class
- Very severe classes flag risk of local tunnel collapse and demand support design attention
- Fine-grained weak rocks (shale, mudstone, flysch) are the candidate lithologies to screen

## Checks

- Confirm unit consistency: 0.027 times depth in metres yields MPa directly
- Confirm the strength value used is rock mass strength, not intact rock strength

## Common errors

- Using intact UCS instead of rock mass strength in the ratio, which greatly understates squeezing
- Unit errors mixing kPa and MPa in the stress estimate

## Caveats

- YouTube lecture — learning material, not an approved design procedure; verify against project specification and current standards
- The strain vs ratio chart and its severity bands are shown on screen — read from the video
- Only vertical gravitational stress is considered; tectonic or horizontal stress effects are not addressed
