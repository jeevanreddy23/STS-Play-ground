# Select a batter surface stabilisation technique from slope and site factors

> **Source:** `guideline-for-batter-surface-stabilisation-using-vegetation.pdf` (Section 1 (pp. 4-5, including the selection flowchart Fig 1.3), Section 2 decision support table (pp. 6-7), Section 3.1 slope gradient and slope length with Tables 3.1.1a and 3.1.1b (pp. 8-10), Section 3.2 flow conditions with Table 3.2 (p. 11), Section 3.11 (p. 18), Section 3.12 cost (p. 19), Section 7 fact sheets 1-15 (pp. 28-57). **Outcome.** A shortlisted and then selected vegetative batter surface stabilisation technique that suits the batter gradient, slope length, flow condition, cost envelope and the date by which erosion protection is needed.

## Inputs

- Surveyed batter gradient expressed as horizontal run to vertical rise, taken from as-constructed survey rather than design
- Slope length measured from batter hinge to toe, including any benches
- Flow condition reaching the batter face: raindrop splash only, sheet flow, or concentrated flow
- Whether upstream overland flow is diverted away by catch drains or benches
- Date by which erosion protection must be effective, and whether the site is being closed down
- Access and safety constraints on the face, and available plant
- Bioregion and any landscape or visual amenity requirement
- Budget covering whole-of-life, not install only

## Procedure

1. Take the governing gradient and the governing slope length as the steepest and longest on the batter; a batter with mixed geometry is assessed on its worst case, though different techniques may be applied concurrently to different parts.
2. Convert the gradient to the guideline's descriptor set (run:rise, percentage, degrees) so it can be read against the slope descriptor table.
3. Check the gradient falls inside the range the guideline covers. Flatter than the flattest tabulated slope, vegetation alone can be used successfully and the choice is driven by the other factors. Steeper than the steepest tabulated slope, vegetation alone will not carry the surface and a mix of vegetative and non-vegetative treatment is needed, or the batter is redesigned flatter.
4. Shortlist candidate techniques from the decision support table using gradient, then read indicative cost band and time-to-erosion-protection band for each.
5. Filter the shortlist by flow condition: techniques suited to raindrop splash only will not survive sheet or concentrated flow, and concentrated flow needs a treatment with structural cover.
6. Filter again by the required timing. If protection is needed immediately (site shutdown, wet season, handover), drop techniques that rely on vegetation establishing and keep those that protect on installation.
7. Filter by access: check that the plant needed to prepare and apply the treatment can work the slope safely.
8. Read the fact sheet for each surviving option for its specific preparation, application and surveillance requirements before committing.
9. Where slope length exceeds what the soil's erodibility will carry, either break the slope with benches or terraces set continuously along the contour, or re-engineer the batter, or step up to a more robust technique.

## Decision rules

- Topsoil should not be placed on slopes steeper than 2h:1v without an additional means of holding it, because of the risk of the topsoil sheet slipping off.
- Maximum topsoil thickness is tied to gradient: the guideline tabulates a reduced thickness for slopes between 3h:1v and 2h:1v, a larger thickness for slopes flatter than 3h:1v, and requires advice for anything steeper than 2h:1v.
- Slopes steeper than 2h:1v are unsafe for foot access on the face without fall attenuation.
- Around 70 per cent soil surface cover is treated as the threshold for effective erosion protection, whether the cover is vegetation, mulch, blanket or a combination.
- Any bench, terrace or catch drain must run continuously along the contour. A control that falls off the contour concentrates flow to a single outlet and makes the erosion worse.
- Whole-of-life cost governs, not install cost. Low-material, low-preparation treatments tend to move cost into rework, weed control and slope repair.
- This is surface stabilisation only. It does not substitute for a geotechnical stability assessment of the batter.

## Checks

- Confirm the gradient used came from accurate site survey including any construction-stage modification, not the design drawing.
- Confirm whether upstream flows are actually diverted; the technique suitability assumes they are.
- Confirm the batter has no ponding on or above it, particularly where dispersive soils are present.
- Confirm a separate geotechnical stability assessment exists for the batter.
- Confirm the selected technique's fact sheet preparation requirements are compatible with the plant and access available.

## Common errors

- Selecting on average gradient rather than the steepest gradient on the batter.
- Assuming upstream flow is diverted when the catch drain is not built or not continuous.
- Choosing a seeding-based technique where erosion protection is required immediately.
- Treating install cost as the whole cost and then paying for rework.
- Reading the decision support table as a final answer instead of a shortlisting step, and skipping the fact sheet.
- Placing full-thickness topsoil on a steep batter and losing it in the first storm.

## Caveats

- The guideline is explicit that it does not address geotechnical or civil engineering aspects of batter stability; a separate geotechnical assessment is always required and the word 'stabilisation' throughout means surface stabilisation.
- Cost bands are indicative at the 2015 date of publication and should be re-priced.
- Written for NSW road batters; species, bioregion advice and contacts are NSW-specific.
