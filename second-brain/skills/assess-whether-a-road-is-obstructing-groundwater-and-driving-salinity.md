# Assess whether a road is obstructing groundwater and driving salinity

> **Source:** `roads-and-salinity.pdf` (The Impact of Roads on Salinity — roads in flat areas and roads in hilly areas, with the road design and groundwater diagrams (pp. 13-14); Assessing Roads for Salinity Damage (pp. 15-16); Assessing Roads for their Impact on Salinity, including the bore transect diagram and the Western Australian case study (pp. 17-18). DIPNR, 2003.), held in the local reference library — see

**Outcome.** A finding on whether an existing or proposed road embankment is acting as a barrier to lateral groundwater or surface water movement, supported by a bore transect and drainage survey, and a view on whether road works or off-road measures are the better remedy.

## Inputs

- Surface relief survey and natural drainage pattern across and along the alignment
- Road crest height and surface contours upslope and downslope
- Culvert locations, sizes and capacities
- A transect of shallow monitoring bores placed above and below the road
- Standing water level readings and their reduced levels
- Terrain setting: flat or hilly, and the position of the road in the slope
- Aerial photography, and where available satellite imagery combined with elevation and water accumulation models
- Evidence of downslope effects: scalding, salt-tolerant vegetation, waterlogging, poor soil health, saline runoff

## Procedure

1. Establish the surface water picture first. Survey surface relief and natural drainage to map how water moves across the site, then compare surface contours upslope against the road crest height to identify where the road, its drainage or its culverts impede flow.
2. Identify the zones of lateral groundwater movement. In hilly terrain the critical zone is the more permeable weathered colluvium above the less permeable rock substrata, where groundwater moves laterally close to the surface.
3. Install a transect of shallow monitoring bores running through the alignment, with bores above the road and at least one below it.
4. Compare the water levels above and below the road against the change in ground elevation between them. If the drop in water table across the road is less than the elevation change would imply, treat the road as acting as a barrier to groundwater movement.
5. Re-read the transect over time. An impeded flow builds up behind the embankment, so the elevated water table on the upslope side develops progressively rather than showing at once.
6. Assess any exposure or release of groundwater over the life of the pavement — deep drains that intercept the water table, or collected and extracted water that has to be disposed of — for its own salinity impact, since it moves saline water into the wider environment.
7. Use aerial photography to identify currently affected areas, and where predictive elevation and water accumulation data exist, compare road elevation against the predicted affected area to flag lengths of road at future risk.
8. Weigh road-based remedies (improved surface drainage, more or larger culverts, a floodway) against off-road remedies (stabilising the affected land upstream, diverting ponded surface water to storage), and pick on long-term durability rather than on which one is inside the road reserve.
9. Where lateral flow assessment or quantification is needed, bring in a hydrogeologist — lateral movement depends on site hydrogeology and is not readable from surface evidence alone.

## Decision rules

- The comparison that identifies a barrier is between the water table drop across the road and the ground elevation drop across the same distance — not the absolute depth to water on either side.
- Deep drains that intercept the water table cut both ways: they may lower the table and dry the subgrade, but they also become direct entry points for surface water into the groundwater system, raising recharge, and they create a saline water disposal problem.
- A road can be the wrong thing to fix. Where an ongoing siltation or flooding process drives the problem, road modifications may not give a long-term solution and upstream land treatment plus surface water diversion may be the better answer.
- Extraction and disposal of groundwater requires development approval under the relevant water and environment protection legislation.
- Assess every proposed road development for its impact on groundwater salinity at minimum, not only those already showing damage.
- No single tool identifies or predicts salinity reliably in all settings; run several and look at the outputs together before concluding.

## Checks

- Confirm the bore transect actually spans the embankment, with a bore genuinely downslope of it.
- Confirm reduced levels for both ground surface and water level are surveyed to a common datum — the whole method is a level comparison.
- Confirm readings cover more than one season before concluding on a build-up.
- Confirm culvert invert levels and capacity, not just culvert presence.
- Confirm whether the salinity signal downslope predates the road, using historical aerial photography.

## Common errors

- Comparing absolute water table depths either side of the road instead of comparing the water table drop against the elevation drop.
- Reading a single round of bore levels and calling it a barrier, or calling it clear.
- Assuming deep drains are a fix without addressing where the intercepted saline water goes.
- Treating a road-reserve solution as the only option when the driving process is upstream.
- Inferring lateral groundwater flow from surface topography without hydrogeological input.

## Caveats

- 2003 publication. Agency names, databases and approval pathways have changed; the assessment method stands but the contacts and legislation references need checking.
- The diagrams are simplified and exclude subsurface geological features that control groundwater flow in real terrain.
- Adds a road-as-barrier assessment method not covered by the existing urban salinity investigation and pavement rehabilitation skills; the damage-assessment side of this document overlaps those and is deliberately not repeated here.
