# Assess excavatability, select excavation plant and allow for bulking

> **Source:** `Handbook of Geotechnical Investigation and Design Tables_Bert Look 2014.pdf` (Chapter 12 Earthworks, Sections 12.1-12.13 (Tables 12.1 to 12.13), pp 159-166), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** A preliminary but defensible call on whether material will be dug, ripped, broken or blasted, with plant sized, production rates estimated and bulking allowed for in the earthworks balance.

## Inputs

- Intact strength (unconfined compression or point load index) and weathering grade
- Discontinuity spacing and orientation, and RQD
- Seismic wave velocity from a refraction survey where available
- Rock type and abrasiveness
- Available plant: dozer class, excavator class, rock breaker, drill and blast capability
- Excavation geometry - open cut, confined excavation, shaft, tunnel

## Procedure

1. Understand the limits of the exercise first. Table 12.2 lists the controlling factors and Look states that strength - the usual single indicator - represents only around a third of the consideration, and that different assessment methods routinely give different answers.
2. Do a first pass with Table 12.3, entering material strength to read the likely excavation requirement (easy dig through to blast). Note Look's cost observation: the step from easy excavation to blasting is roughly a twentyfold cost factor, so this call carries commercial weight and should be qualified accordingly.
3. Refine with strength plus fracture. Table 12.4 gives excavation characteristics from hardness and strength; Table 12.5 combines strength with discontinuity spacing (Franklin chart lineage) to place the material in dig, rip, break or blast; Table 12.6 updates that for heavy modern ripping equipment.
4. Where a seismic survey exists, cross-check with Table 12.7, which maps seismic wave velocity to excavatability. Rippability by velocity is rock-type dependent, so use it as a second opinion rather than the primary basis.
5. Estimate production. Table 12.8 gives production rates by rock type and plant, developed because the rating methods correlate poorly with actual output; the rates allow for turning, pushing and a share of dozer time spent push-loading scrapers, and require a conversion factor to compare with manufacturer handbook figures.
6. Where an excavator rather than a dozer will do the work, use the diggability index (Table 12.9), which brings in weathering, and read the required plant class from Table 12.10.
7. For confined excavation - drilled shafts, shafts, tunnels - use Table 12.11. It sets out the changeover points: earth auger to rock auger to down-the-hole hammer for bored piers, and backhoe to road header to drill-and-blast or disc-cutter TBM for shielded tunnelling.
8. Where a numerical rating is wanted, apply the Weaver rippability rating chart (Table 12.12), which combines strength, discontinuity spacing and orientation, plant capability and joint condition. Look notes it is dated relative to current plant sizes, and that ratings above a stated threshold should be regarded as requiring pre-blasting.
9. Allow for bulking with Table 12.13: separate the transportation bulking (excavation to transport, a modest percentage for soils and soft rock, more for hard rock) from the volume change between in-situ and compacted states, and add a typical wastage allowance. Get this into the cut-fill balance before earthworks quantities are priced.
10. Cross-check the excavatability call against the Chapter 6 drilling-rig refusal tables - what the investigation rig could and could not penetrate is direct evidence about the same question.

## Decision rules

- No single method is authoritative; run at least two of the strength-spacing, seismic velocity and rating-chart routes and report the disagreement.
- The geological definition of rock and the contractual definition of rock are different - state which is being used in any excavatability statement that will be priced.
- Blasting in these tables includes rock breaking as a difficulty class, not necessarily explosives.
- Ratings above the chart threshold indicate the material is not rippable without pre-blasting regardless of plant size.
- Confined excavation shifts the changeover points earlier than open-cut assessment implies - never apply open-cut rippability to a shaft or a bored pier.

## Checks

- Excavatability assessed by at least two independent routes
- Seismic velocity interpretation checked against rock type dependence
- Production rates converted consistently before comparison with plant handbook data
- Bulking factors applied separately for transport and for compaction in the earthworks balance
- Assessment cross-checked against investigation drilling refusal behaviour

## Common errors

- Quoting a single excavation classification from strength alone in a document that will be priced
- Applying open-cut rippability charts to bored pier or shaft excavation
- Using manufacturer production rates without the stated conversion
- Omitting bulking from the cut-fill balance and finding a spoil shortfall or surplus late
- Ignoring joint orientation, which the rating charts treat as significant

## Caveats

- Reference text, not a design standard - verify against the source pages and Australian practice before use.
- Charts are extracted approximations from published figures and the plant sizes assumed are dated.
- Overlaps the project's existing rock excavation skills sourced from other references - use both and reconcile.
