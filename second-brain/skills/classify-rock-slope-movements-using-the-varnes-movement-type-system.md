# Classify rock slope movements using the Varnes movement-type system

> **Source:** [YouTube — Why Slopes Fail: The Varnes Rockslide Classification System](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3wiBE5rV6pM&t=0s) (00:00-08:09). A YouTube lecture — learning

**Outcome.** A movement-type classification (fall, topple, planar or rotational slide, lateral spread, creep, or complex) for a rock slope, with the diagnostic field evidence for each

## Inputs

- Field observations: discontinuity/bedding orientation, weathering state, presence of tension cracks, failure plane geometry, movement rate
- Site history: earthquake exposure, monitoring records
- Indirect creep indicators: broken fences, curved or tilted tree trunks

## Procedure

1. Classify by type of movement, following the Varnes scheme for bedrock: fall, topple, slide (rotational or translational), spread, flow/creep
2. Identify toppling where steeply inclined bedding or steep discontinuities lack friction between layers, letting columns rotate outward under gravity
3. Identify planar (translational) sliding where a persistent discontinuity set parallel to the slope daylights at the face; a tension crack behind the crest is an early warning indicator
4. Identify rotational sliding where heavy weathering and soil-rock mixtures produce a circular rather than planar failure surface
5. Identify lateral spread where a very large, generally competent rock mass sits on a thin weak layer (typically clay), the basal plane is very gentle (usually under about 10 degrees), and failure is triggered by strong earthquakes rather than rainfall
6. Identify creep where movement is very slow, no defined failure plane exists yet (unconnected cracks), and the mass is often foliated metamorphic rock (e.g. schist) where strain concentrates along foliation
7. Use complex as the class when the movement combines or transitions between two or more types
8. Confirm creep in the field from bent/curved tree trunks and broken fences, and recognise that acceleration begins once the failure plane finally connects

## Decision rules

- Daylighting discontinuity plus tension crack points to planar slide potential
- Gentle basal plane under a huge rock mass on saturated clay plus seismic trigger points to lateral spread; rainfall alone will not trigger it
- No defined failure plane plus very slow multi-year movement points to creep, which can remain safe for decades until the plane connects
- When the mechanism is ambiguous or evolving, classify as complex rather than forcing a single type

## Checks

- Verify the inferred failure surface shape (planar vs circular) against weathering condition
- Check trigger consistency: earthquake-dependent mechanisms should correlate with regional seismicity

## Common errors

- Calling a rotational failure planar in heavily weathered soil-rock mixtures
- Dismissing creep because movement is slow, then missing the acceleration once a through-going failure plane forms
- Overlooking the thin weak clay layer that controls lateral spreads because the visible mass is strong rock

## Caveats

- YouTube lecture — learning material, not an approved design procedure; verify against project specification and current standards
- Lecture covers only the bedrock branch of the classification; soil landslides are excluded
- Case-study details (e.g. the Japanese Aratazawa landslide) are anecdotal context, not procedure
