# Assess sodic soils and prescribe gypsum, lime and tillage treatment

> **Source:** `Sodic-soil-management.pdf` (Vegetable SOILpak Chapter D5, pp. D5.1-D5.5 (Table D5-1 on p. D5.3)), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** A sodicity diagnosis (ESP-based) and a treatment plan (gypsum rate, lime blend, tillage discipline, ripping strategy) that restores structure, infiltration and drainage without creating new salinity problems.

## Inputs

- Dispersion test score and chemical soil test giving exchangeable cations and CEC (to compute ESP)
- Soil pH (CaCl2)
- Profile knowledge: depth of non-sodic topsoil, soil type (self-mulching clay, red brown earth, etc.)
- Irrigation water quality if bore/saline water is in use

## Procedure

1. Diagnose: sodicity is exchangeable sodium as a proportion of cations on clay (ESP); structural problems begin at roughly ESP above ~5. Expect symptoms of swelling/dispersion: surface crusting, low infiltration and hydraulic conductivity, hard dense subsoils, waterlogging, restricted rooting, tunnel/gully erosion.
2. Screen with a dispersion test; a dispersion score of about 6-16 flags a potentially gypsum-responsive soil — then run the chemical test to calculate ESP before committing to treatment.
3. Set gypsum rate from surface-soil ESP (source Table D5-1): ESP greater than 5 and less than 10, apply 2-5 t/ha; ESP greater than 10, apply 5 t/ha.
4. Run test strips first (e.g. 2.5 and 5 t/ha, plus an untreated strip) and judge response by friability, tillage draft, infiltration, waterlogging and seedling emergence before whole-paddock application.
5. Choose the calcium source by pH (CaCl2): lime is largely insoluble above pH 5 (little benefit); below pH 5 lime treats both acidity and sodicity; in the pH 5-6.5 range a lime-gypsum mix can outlast gypsum alone.
6. For sodic subsoils, combine high-rate gypsum (5-10 t/ha) with deep ripping, ideally concentrating gypsum in the rip lines; expect to repeat every 2-3 years as gypsum leaches; use non-inversion tillage to keep sodic subsoil at depth and preserve topsoil organic matter; cultivate sodic soils minimally.
7. Time surface gypsum so rain/irrigation has not leached it from the seedbed by sowing, where surface crusting is the target problem.

## Decision rules

- If the non-sodic topsoil is deeper than about 15-20 cm, a gypsum response is unlikely — the existing structure already supports growth; target subsoil constraints instead.
- Gypsum works two ways (slightly raising soil-solution salinity to suppress swelling, and Ca-for-Na cation exchange); saline water mimics the first mechanism, but avoid saline irrigation as a fix — it raises soil salinity and often sodicity, and later fresh-water leaching of a sodic profile collapses structure.
- A sodic soil can appear well-structured while salinity keeps it flocculated — assess sodicity and salinity together (the ESP vs EC flocculation relationship) before predicting behaviour under changed water quality.
- Allow months for gypsum to leach into subsoils before judging failure.

## Checks

- ESP computed from lab data, not inferred from dispersion score alone.
- Untreated control strip retained for comparison.
- pH (CaCl2) measured before choosing lime or blends.
- Re-inspection scheduled (ripping/gypsum benefits fade in 2-3 years).

## Common errors

- Applying gypsum to deep non-sodic topsoils and seeing no response.
- Aggressive or inversion tillage that lifts sodic subsoil and destroys organic matter.
- Confusing sodicity (exchangeable Na fraction) with salinity (soluble salt concentration) — they need different tests and different responses.
- Using sodic/saline bore water for irrigation and importing the problem.

## Caveats

- Classification and aggressivity calls must be verified against the source document and the project spec; rates here are agronomic guidance for irrigated vegetable soils, not geotechnical specification values.
- ESP interpretation bands differ between sources: this chapter treats ~5% as the onset of problems, while booklet3siteinvestigationsforurbansalinity (2).pdf (p. 14, secondary source for the interpretation step) rates <5 non-sodic, 5-15 sodic, >15 highly sodic.
- For engineering earthworks on sodic/dispersive soils (erosion, tunnelling, compaction behaviour), supplement with the Emerson-class interpretation in the urban salinity booklet rather than agronomic response alone.
