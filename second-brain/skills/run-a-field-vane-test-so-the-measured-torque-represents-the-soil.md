# Run a field vane test so the measured torque represents the soil

> **Source:** `In Situ Testing in Geomechanics The Main Tests by Fernando Schnaid (z-lib.org).pdf` (Chapter 4 Vane test - General considerations (pp 151-152), Equipment and procedures (pp 152-157, Figures 4.1 to 4.6)), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** Vane data in which rod friction, insertion disturbance, time at rest, blade thickness and rotation rate have all been controlled or measured, so the peak and remoulded torques mean what the interpretation assumes.

## Inputs

- Vane blade dimensions: height, diameter, aspect ratio and blade thickness
- Insertion method - from the base of a borehole, or pushed within a protective housing
- Borehole diameter or housing outside diameter, to set the required advance below it
- Torque measurement device and its resolution
- Expected undrained strength range and drainage characteristics of the layer
- Rod friction calibration record

## Procedure

1. Confirm the material suits the test. The vane is for saturated clay and other fine-grained low-permeability materials - silts, organic peats, tailings - up to a moderate strength ceiling. It does not apply to sand, gravel or other highly permeable soils, which drain partially at the standard shearing rate. Establish the drainage characteristics of the layer before testing, not after.
2. Choose the insertion method. From the base of a borehole, advance the vane below the hole bottom by at least a stated multiple of the borehole outside diameter so the tested soil is outside the disturbed zone. Within a protective housing, stop the driving above the test depth with the vane inside the housing, then extend the blades into undisturbed ground by at least the same multiple of the housing outside diameter.
3. Calibrate rod friction. Where the vane runs inside a casing or housing, friction losses must be measured, not assumed - insert the torque rods to each test depth without the vane attached and record the torque needed to rotate them, and subtract that from the test torque. The book states this calibration is mandatory.
4. Use a standard blade geometry. An aspect ratio of height to diameter of two is now standard practice, with blade heights in a conventional range; smaller blades are used in stiffer soils to keep torque within the instrument's range and larger blades in soft clay to improve resolution.
5. Keep the blade thin. Blade thickness controls the disturbance created by pushing the vane in, and measured strength falls systematically as the perimeter ratio - a function of blade thickness over vane diameter - increases. The book notes that even a standard-thickness blade underestimates the undisturbed in-situ strength by a material margin relative to the value extrapolated to zero blade thickness. Use the thinnest blade the strength range permits, and record the thickness.
6. Control the time between insertion and rotation. Pore pressures generated by insertion dissipate with time, consolidating the soil around the blades and increasing the measured strength; the reported increase is small over half an hour and substantial over days. The standard short waiting period exists so that the strength gain from consolidation does not exceed the strength loss from insertion disturbance, producing a conservative estimate. Do not let the interval drift.
7. Rotate at the standard rate within its tolerance and record the full torque against rotation relationship, not just the peak. The peak torque gives the peak undrained strength.
8. After the peak, rotate the vane through a substantial number of complete revolutions to fully remould the failure surface, then measure the residual torque to obtain the remoulded strength and hence the sensitivity.
9. Where anisotropy is to be assessed, run vanes of different aspect ratio at the same depth. The torque contributions on the vertical cylindrical surface and on the horizontal end planes can then be separated, but the angular rotation rate must be adjusted between blade sizes so that the peripheral velocity does not itself change the measured strength.
10. Record the torque resolution of the device relative to the expected strength - the achievable resolution in computed shear strength is small but not negligible in very soft deposits.
11. Consider the platform: the blade can be mounted in a frame with a drive engine for tests in pre-drilled holes, or integrated within a cone penetration testing unit that drives to the selected depth without pre-drilling, which removes the borehole disturbance entirely.

## Decision rules

- Do not use the vane in materials that will drain partially at the standard rotation rate.
- Rod friction must be measured at every test depth, not assumed constant.
- Keep the time between insertion and rotation short and consistent, and record it.
- Use the thinnest practical blade and record the thickness - it biases the result systematically.
- Report peak and remoulded strength together; sensitivity is part of the result, not an extra.

## Checks

- Advance below the borehole or housing verified against the required multiple of diameter
- Rod friction calibration record present for each depth
- Time from insertion to rotation logged for every test
- Rotation rate within tolerance
- Blade dimensions and thickness recorded on the test sheet
- Full torque-rotation curve retained, not only the peak value

## Common errors

- Testing too close to the base of the borehole and measuring disturbed soil
- Ignoring rod friction in cased holes and overstating strength
- Letting the insertion-to-rotation interval stretch and reading a consolidated strength
- Using a thick or damaged blade and accepting the reduced strength as conservative
- Insufficient revolutions before the remoulded reading, giving an overstated remoulded strength and understated sensitivity

## Caveats

- Reference text, not a design standard - verify against the source pages and Australian practice before use.
- The standard blade thickness itself biases the measured strength downward relative to a zero-thickness extrapolation.
- The short standard waiting time is a convention chosen to keep the result conservative, not a physically exact condition.
