# Size retaining walls, soil nails and ground anchors from the Look Chapter 20 preliminary design tables

> **Source:** `Handbook of Geotechnical Investigation and Design Tables_Bert Look 2014.pdf` (Chapter 20 Retaining walls, Sections 20.1-20.23 (Tables 20.1 to 20.23, Figures 20.1 to 20.4), pp 293-307; cross-reference Chapter 23 Sections 23.9, 23.15-23.17), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** Preliminary wall dimensions, embedment, pier spacing, drainage, nail or anchor layout, facing thickness and bond lengths - enough to price and to check a specialist design against, with the limits of each table stated.

## Inputs

- Wall height, whether cut or fill, and the slope above and below the wall
- Soil and rock parameters behind, beneath and in front of the wall
- Groundwater regime, catchment above the wall and design rainfall intensity
- Wall type under consideration and any movement constraint on adjacent assets
- Design life and aggressivity of the ground for metallic elements
- For rock cuts: joint orientation relative to the face and rockfall exposure

## Procedure

1. Classify the retention system with Table 20.1 and note whether the wall will be free-standing, braced or tied back. Use the economics in Section 20.2: gravity and concrete walls tend to be economical below a modest height, reinforced soil walls above it, and precast facing panels can cost as much as the structure they face.
2. Size a gravity wall preliminarily with Table 20.2, and apply a face batter for major walls in an active state so that the movement needed to mobilise the active state is not visually alarming. Adjust for the slope behind and the slope and embedment in front with Table 20.3, both of which change the required dimensions materially.
3. For embedded walls take embedment from Table 20.4 as a multiple of the free-standing height for the soil type, surcharge and whether the wall is propped, and take pier spacing from Table 20.5 as a multiple of pier diameter based on the soil's ability to arch - noting that without some clay content and with a high water table the spacing must reduce.
4. Design the drainage before the structure. Table 20.6 gives typical wall drainage measures by wall type with drainage layer thicknesses, internal drainage systems, filter material and horizontal drain lengths and diameters. Increase pipe sizes for high rainfall intensity or large contributing catchments, provide clean-out access at regular intervals, use a geotextile filter behind the drainage gravel, and avoid compacting hard against the back of the wall.
5. For reinforced soil structures set the minimum embedment from Table 20.7 as a fraction of wall height, with abutments deeper than walls, plus a minimum absolute embedment for shrinkage and a further allowance for scour or future trenching; where the ground slopes away, provide a horizontal bench in front.
6. Take reinforced soil design parameters from Table 20.8: the earth pressure coefficient varies with depth from near at-rest at the crest toward active at depth, and the reinforcement type (rigid inclusions versus extensible) changes the profile; the table also gives the soil-to-reinforcement interface friction. Locate the potential failure surface with Table 20.9 - inextensible reinforcement gives an active zone close to the face, extensible reinforcement a different geometry.
7. Apply corrosion allowances to metallic reinforcement (Table 20.10) and to soil nails (Table 20.16) as a sacrificial thickness over the design life, noting that for driven nails a barrier coating is not practical.
8. For reinforced slopes, use Table 20.11 to keep the two valid but different definitions of factor of safety distinct, and Table 20.12 to select slope facing by batter angle - a facing is needed both for stability appearance and for erosion control.
9. For rock cuttings, choose wall type and facing from Table 20.13 based on stability governed by joint orientation, with a note that the achievable slope is governed by rock strength, joint orientation and rock type, that maintenance berms may be needed on steeper faces, and that rockfall fences or netting may be required at any slope.
10. For soil nails, take preliminary geometry from Table 20.14 (drilled and grouted) or Table 20.15 (driven), noting drilled and grouted nails have larger surface area and higher capacity, that the excavation proceeds in lifts of the order of a metre with nails installed before the next lift, that in low-cohesion soils the shotcrete goes on before the nails, and that lengths increase in high plasticity clays. Driven nails are unsuitable in weak rock and boulder fills.
11. Design the facing with Table 20.17 using the maximum nail service tension and the governing nail spacing, and set shotcrete thickness from Table 20.18 by load and slope angle. Nails are designed to a stated overall factor of safety against pull-out.
12. Where horizontal movement must be limited, move to an anchored wall. Table 20.19 compares nails and anchored walls and their facings; Look records that soil nailing can cost around half a tieback wall but permits greater movement. Take anchor loads from Table 20.20 for the stated anchor inclination and factor of safety, ultimate load transfer values in soil from Table 20.21 (with the note that SPT values there are overburden corrected), rock anchor bond stress from Table 20.22 with the stated factor of safety on ultimate and a reduction for fractured or weathered rock, and bond and unbonded lengths from Table 20.23 with the stated minimum anchor and unbonded lengths.
13. Check wall movements against Chapter 23 - Tables 23.9, 23.15, 23.16 and 23.17 give expected soil nail wall displacements, tolerable displacements for slopes and walls, and observed settlements behind excavations by support system.

## Decision rules

- Every wall gets a drainage system, including walls above the water table.
- Do not compact heavily immediately behind a wall - use light plant and accept the reduced density in that zone, with a drainage layer in place.
- Anchor and nail capacity values are for preliminary design only; proof and acceptance testing on site governs.
- Bond uniformity is not achieved along an anchor bond length - do not extrapolate capacity linearly with length beyond the tabulated range.
- Minimum unbonded lengths exist to prevent unacceptable load loss - do not shorten them to suit a site constraint without a specialist check.
- Where movement of adjacent assets governs, a nailed wall is not equivalent to a tieback wall regardless of the calculated factor of safety.

## Checks

- Drainage sized for the actual catchment and rainfall intensity, with clean-outs and outlets located
- Embedment includes allowances for scour, future trenching and shrinkage
- Sacrificial thickness for the design life and ground aggressivity
- Global stability checked independently of internal wall design
- Anchor and nail preliminary capacities confirmed by on-site testing before construction proceeds
- Predicted wall movement compared against the tolerance of adjacent structures and services

## Common errors

- Designing the structure and adding drainage afterwards
- Using the tabulated anchor load transfer values as design capacities without site testing
- Applying reinforced soil wall parameters intended for inextensible reinforcement to a geogrid wall
- Ignoring the slope above or below the wall in preliminary sizing
- Omitting corrosion allowance on driven nails where a barrier is not achievable
- Selecting soil nailing on cost where the movement it permits is unacceptable to neighbours

## Caveats

- Reference text, not a design standard - verify against the source pages and Australian practice before use.
- The project's existing reinforced soil wall skills (external stability, sizing, reinforced fill specification) are specification-driven; this skill is the preliminary-design counterpart and the specification governs.
- Anchor and nail values are presumptive; proof testing on site is the only acceptable basis for final capacity.
