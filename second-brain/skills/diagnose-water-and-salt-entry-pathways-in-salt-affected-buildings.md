# Diagnose water and salt entry pathways in salt-affected buildings

> **Source:** `building-in-saline-environment-080145.pdf` (pp. 3-8 (Sources of Salt, Sources of Water, Infiltration Rates, Reactions of Salts with Building Materials)), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** Identification of the water source(s), transport mechanism and attack mechanism driving salt damage, so that remediation targets cause rather than symptom.

## Inputs

- Building inspection access (walls, subfloor, roof drainage, surrounding ground levels)
- Knowledge of building materials present (brick, mortar, render, concrete, reinforcement)
- Optional salt-type analysis of efflorescence/soil

## Procedure

1. Classify the water source: rising damp (soil moisture drawn up by capillary action — sources may be on-site, neighbouring, or a district-wide watertable), falling damp (leaking gutters, downpipes, roofs wetting walls/cavities), or condensation (moist indoor air on cool surfaces).
2. Read the tide mark: its height is where evaporation rate balances water supply through the wall — a rising or high tide mark means more water supply or less evaporation.
3. Assess material transport properties: distinguish porosity from permeability (a porous material with closed/isolated pores may still be impermeable); mortar and brick pores of ~0.1-10 um act as capillary tubes, as do fine concrete cracks.
4. Identify the attack mechanism: physical (crystallisation and hydration cycles on wetting/drying exert pressure, fretting material grain by grain — some salts keep attracting moisture from humid air even after water sources are fixed), chemical (acids leach calcium hydroxide from concrete; sulphates react expansively with hydrated calcium aluminate; carbonates lower paste alkalinity from ~pH 12 to ~9.5), or reinforcement corrosion (initiation by carbonation/chloride ionisation, then propagation governed by oxygen, moisture and remaining alkalinity).
5. Check for evaporation-concentration hot spots (e.g. just above a low-permeability render, north-facing external walls) where salts will accumulate fastest.
6. Trace the salt source separately from the water source: salts can come from the ground, groundwater, building materials themselves (unwashed sand, new bricks, magnesite floor toppings) or coatings.

## Decision rules

- If damage continues after water sources are controlled, suspect hygroscopic salts and slow watertable response rather than a missed leak.
- Deep salty soil layers are unlikely to matter unless a water pathway or cut/fill brings them into play.
- Low-permeability renders can worsen brick damage by pushing the evaporation zone (and salt concentration) higher up the wall.
- Chlorides mainly threaten reinforcement (physical crystal damage aside); sulphates mainly threaten the concrete matrix — the dominant salt type changes the remedy.

## Checks

- All plausible water sources surveyed (pipes, pools, irrigation, gutters, ground levels, groundwater) before attributing to one.
- Damage pattern (below vs above DPC, base of wall vs cavity, external vs internal) is consistent with the diagnosed pathway.
- Salt type identified where remediation cost justifies analysis.

## Common errors

- Treating strength as the only durability proxy and ignoring wetting/drying cycle concentration effects.
- Sealing a wall surface without addressing the water source, relocating rather than stopping the damage.
- Overlooking building-material-borne salts (magnesite, unwashed sand, new bricks) and blaming the ground.

## Caveats

- Classification and aggressivity calls must be verified against the source document and the project spec.
- Mechanism detail merged with INDUSTRY_GUIDE_T56_Residential_Slabs_and_Footings_in_Saline_Environments.pdf Section 4 (physical/chemical attack and reinforcement corrosion), which is the secondary source here.
- Pore-size and capillary figures are illustrative theory, not design values.
