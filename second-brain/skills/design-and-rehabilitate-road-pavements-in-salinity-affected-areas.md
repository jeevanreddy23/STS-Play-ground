# Design and rehabilitate road pavements in salinity-affected areas

> **Source:** `Techniques-to-use-on-roads-affected-by-salinity.pdf` (Sections 2-5 (pp. 2-13), conclusions pp. 14-15), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** Road designs and rehabilitation strategies that keep water out of the pavement and subgrade ('umbrella' approach), resist saline attack, and avoid the road itself worsening salinity.

## Inputs

- Road class and traffic (access place through collector/highway), existing distress survey
- Watertable depth and salinity data along the alignment
- Subgrade properties: soaked/unsoaked CBR, Plasticity Index, swell potential, permeability
- Drainage layout options (kerb and gutter, table drains, subsurface drains)

## Procedure

1. Apply the 'umbrella' principle: crown/levels and a low-permeability surfacing (sprayed seal, asphalt or concrete) shed water to drainage inlets so it cannot enter pavement layers or subgrade.
2. For salinity-prone new or rehabilitated roads use the four recognised strategies: raise the pavement level with suitable material and stabilise upper layers; stabilise the subgrade to cut moisture susceptibility; design subsurface drainage; and plant deep-rooted vegetation in the reserve to lower the watertable (balancing roadside safety).
3. Assess the moisture regime at design (per Austroads pavement design guidance): rainfall/evaporation, subgrade reactivity, surface permeability, watertable depth, layer permeability contrast, shoulder sealing, verge/median vegetation, boxed vs full-width construction, and drainage provisions.
4. Where the pavement (typically 400-500 mm deep on local roads) can dam downslope groundwater movement, place subsurface drains orthogonal to the centreline beneath the stabilised subgrade to let groundwater pass; in cuttings, establish watertable height and drain away from the cut to stop saturation.
5. Classify expansive subgrades (guide bands: very high at LL >70, PI >45 and PI-weighted fines >3200; low at LL <50, PI <25, <1200) and treat (stabilise/moisture-seal) accordingly since shape loss follows seasonal moisture change.
6. Upgrade kerb and gutter in saline areas: typical extruded kerb concrete (~15 MPa, modest compaction) underperforms against saline moisture compared with ~32 MPa highway concrete — raise strength and compaction standards or accept programmed replacement.
7. Avoid porous/permeable segmental paving in salinity-affected areas (defeats the umbrella); for concrete roads, repair and seal unplanned cracks (e.g. cross-stitching), provide subsurface drainage under continuously reinforced pavements whose fine cracks cannot be sealed, and consider epoxy-coated tie bars/thicker slabs where steel joint hardware may corrode.

## Decision rules

- If a network built to standard details shows widespread early distress, upgrade the standard details (the Wyndham approach: standard configurations with lime-stabilised clay subgrades per traffic tier) rather than patching failures piecemeal.
- Low-permeability subgrade layers concentrate water and salt — pair any low-permeability formation layer with edge subsurface drains to remove perched water.
- Where salinity risk is extreme, the correct engineering decision may be planning-level: avoid or limit development/rezoning rather than over-engineering roads.
- Recycled or crushed sandstone materials with high permeability let water through to weak wet subgrades — screen materials for permeability, not just strength.

## Checks

- Surface maintained free of ponding (also a hydroplaning hazard) and cracks sealed promptly.
- Subsurface drain grades sufficient to prevent silting and maintain flow (a commonly underestimated failure).
- Sealing aggregates and construction water meet salt limits (RTA-era limits cited: maximum ~600 ppm chloride and ~400 ppm sulphate ions).
- Capping layers meet the specified permeability (VicRoads example: <5 x 10^-9 m/s at OMC +/-5%, 98% +/-1% standard density ratio).

## Common errors

- Treating salinity distress as ordinary traffic wear and resurfacing without addressing water movement.
- Using standard kerb mixes in saline areas and facing wholesale early replacement.
- Ignoring the road's own impact on salinity (damming groundwater upslope, focusing infiltration).
- Relying on infiltration-based stormwater measures in salinity areas where recharge is the problem.

## Caveats

- Classification and aggressivity calls must be verified against the source document, current Austroads/agency specifications and the project spec — several cited guides were drafts or have been superseded since 2005.
- Secondary source roads-and-salinity.pdf (DIPNR 2003) covers the same ground (construction, damage costing ~940 km of NSW road at risk in 2000 rising to ~4850 km by 2050, assessment and treatment) but its text extraction is OCR-corrupted, so its detailed content could not be independently paraphrased — verify any use against a clean copy.
- Expansive-soil bands and permeability values are guide figures from the cited tables, not universal limits.
