# Forecast time to rock slope collapse from displacement monitoring data

> **Source:** [YouTube — Can We Predict Rock Slope Collapse? Using Monitoring Data](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9Idq3XYHMZY&t=0s) (00:01-06:38). A YouTube lecture — learning
> material, not an approved design procedure. Equations and numbers were
> heard through auto-captions; read them off the video before use, and
> verify anything design-relevant against the standard and the project
> specification. Engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** An empirical estimate of the number of days until collapse of an accelerating rock slide, from the onset velocity of progressive movement

## Inputs

- Long-term displacement/velocity monitoring record
- Identified transition point from regressive (decelerating/steady) to progressive (accelerating) movement and the onset velocity v0 at that point
- Empirical constant K, estimated as the ratio between mid-record and initial velocities (previous studies suggest a magnitude around 7; the example used 7.25)
- Velocity-time chart with logarithmic velocity axis

## Procedure

1. Plot monitored velocity against time and locate the change in curve character marking the start of the progressive stage; record the onset velocity v0 (example: 0.01 m/day)
2. Estimate K from the monitoring record as the ratio of a mid-record velocity to the initial velocity (example magnitude about 7.25)
3. Compute the collapse velocity as K squared times the onset velocity (example: 7.25 squared times 0.01 gives about 0.53 m/day)
4. Enter the log-scale velocity axis of the monitoring chart at the collapse velocity and read across/down to the corresponding time (example: about 61 days from onset)
5. Use the forecast to plan monitoring intensification, access restrictions and next steps rather than as a precise date

## Decision rules

- Regressive-stage movement alone does not warrant collapse forecasting; the method applies once progressive acceleration is established
- Treat the forecast as an order-of-magnitude planning tool: the correlation is highly empirical and will not hold in all cases

## Checks

- Confirm the trend of the progressive stage is well established (near-linear on the chart) before extrapolating
- Read the log velocity axis carefully; small reading errors change the forecast substantially

## Common errors

- Picking v0 from an arbitrary point instead of the regressive-to-progressive transition
- Treating the empirical K as universal rather than checking it against the site's own monitoring ratio
- Reporting the forecast date as deterministic to stakeholders

## Caveats

- YouTube lecture — learning material, not an approved design procedure; verify against project specification and current standards
- Method is empirical, derived from a small number of open-pit case studies; the lecturer repeatedly warns it may not apply everywhere
- The exact form of the velocity formulas and the sign convention for K are shown on screen — read from the video; captions garble them
