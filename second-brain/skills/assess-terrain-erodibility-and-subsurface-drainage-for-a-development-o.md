# Assess terrain, erodibility and subsurface drainage for a development or alignment

> **Source:** `Handbook of Geotechnical Investigation and Design Tables_Bert Look 2014.pdf` (Chapter 15 Terrain assessment, drainage and erosion, Sections 15.1-15.32 (Tables 15.1 to 15.32, Figures 15.1 to 15.4), pp 219-236), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** A terrain assessment at the right scale, an erodibility and dispersivity classification, erosion controls sized to the slope and catchment, and subsurface drainage designed for the soil type and flow.

## Inputs

- Aerial photography at appropriate scale, geological and topographic mapping
- Slope gradients, slope lengths and slope shape (linear or concave)
- Soil classification, plasticity, dispersivity test results (Emerson or equivalent)
- Rainfall and catchment characteristics
- Permeability of the soils to be drained
- Development type and required grades

## Procedure

1. Do the terrain evaluation before ground truthing. Table 15.1 lists the considerations, and Table 15.2 ranks aerial photograph scales for their suitability to recognise instability - instability of a given size is only visible at a matching scale, so choose imagery scale to the feature size being looked for, not to what is available.
2. Screen the site by gradient. Table 15.3 gives grades required by development type, Table 15.5 gives development procedures by slope gradient alone, and Table 15.6 categorises terrain. Table 15.4 converts rolling resistance to an equivalent gradient for wheeled plant - which is why haul road maintenance reduces operating cost and why a haul road grade must be assessed together with its surface condition.
3. Classify any observed instability. Table 15.7 gives typical landslide dimensions in soils and Table 15.8 the velocity scale, which is the primary determinant of damage and loss of life. Rockfall is treated separately in Chapter 14.
4. Assess erodibility. Table 15.9 relates erodibility to grade, Table 15.10 gives a simplified hierarchy by USC soil type, and Table 15.11 sets out the universal soil loss equation factors - developed for agriculture, so useful for the governing elements rather than for direct quantitative use. Note vegetation both reduces erosion and reinforces the soil with roots while drying it.
5. Test for dispersivity where clay is involved (Table 15.12). Look's rule is the same as for other index tests: do not judge dispersivity on one poor result; combine it with site observations of gullying and tunnel erosion.
6. Locate where erosion will initiate using Table 15.13, which places stability fields in terms of slope and contributing area per unit contour length for a stated hydrologic condition - erosion concentrates where contributing area and slope combine, not uniformly across the batter.
7. Shape the slope. Table 15.14 shows sediment loss is substantially lower from a concave profile than from a linear one of the same height - reshaping is a design control, not just a landscaping choice.
8. Set permissible velocities. Table 15.15 gives erosion velocities by material based on particle size, with the observation that fine to medium sands and silts are the most erodible, especially uniformly graded; Table 15.16 gives competent mean velocities allowing for depth of flow in channels. Hard, high plasticity clays and very dense sands tolerate higher velocities.
9. Select controls from Table 15.17 by site size and slope, add vegetation buffers and contour drains, and bench the slope per Table 15.18 - a reverse cross-fall on the bench, a minimum bench depth, and a bench width that also satisfies any rockfall catchment requirement.
10. Design subsurface drainage. Size perforated pipe underdrains with Table 15.19, set spacing and depth by soil permeability with Table 15.20, and observe the minimum grades, minimum trench width, minimum cover in soil and rock, and maximum outlet spacing that Look states. Aggregate drains are sized with Tables 15.24 to 15.26, where larger aggregate gives a substantially greater equivalent capacity.
11. For dams, channels and embankments add the seepage controls: chimney drain slopes (Table 15.27), drainage blankets (Table 15.28), piping resistance (Table 15.29), filter design (Table 15.30, with geotextile filters cross-referenced to Chapter 16), earth dam seepage loss guidance (Table 15.31) and clay blanket thickness (Table 15.32). Apply the higher drainage factors of safety in Table 15.23, which reflect the greater consequence and uncertainty in drainage performance.
12. For unlined channels take seepage rates from Table 15.21 and compare open channel flow with seepage using Table 15.22, noting the water-tight channel benchmark and typical concrete lining thickness.

## Decision rules

- Match the aerial photograph scale to the size of the instability being sought - a smaller scale simply will not show it.
- Erosion assessment is a combination of soil type, gradient, slope length, slope shape and contributing area; a soil classification alone is not an erodibility assessment.
- Dispersive soils require filter criteria that are more conservative than for non-dispersive soils; medium and high plasticity non-dispersive clays can use relaxed criteria.
- Drainage measures carry higher factors of safety than structural elements because their long-term performance is less certain.
- Filter thickness should exceed a multiple of the maximum particle size, and filter permeability should exceed that of the soil being filtered by roughly an order of magnitude.

## Checks

- Slope shape assessed - reshaping to a concave profile considered before adding hard controls
- Dispersivity tested where clay batters or embankment cores are involved
- Underdrain grades against the desirable and minimum values, and outlet spacing against the maximum
- Filter compatibility checked in both directions (retention and permeability)
- Channel design velocities checked against both material-based and depth-of-flow-based limits
- Bench geometry satisfies drainage, maintenance access and rockfall catchment simultaneously

## Common errors

- Assessing terrain instability on imagery at the wrong scale and concluding the site is stable
- Using the universal soil loss equation quantitatively outside its agricultural calibration
- Designing a filter without testing the base soil for dispersivity
- Setting underdrain grades below the minimum and getting siltation
- Treating vegetation as the whole erosion control on a long uninterrupted batter

## Caveats

- Reference text, not a design standard - verify against the source pages and Australian practice before use.
- Erosion threshold data is stated for one hydrologic condition and will shift with rainfall regime.
- Seepage loss guidance for earth dams is compiled and interpolated by the author from vague published sources - treat as indicative only.
