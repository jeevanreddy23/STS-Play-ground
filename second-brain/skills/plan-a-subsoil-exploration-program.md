# Plan a subsoil exploration program

> **Source:** `Principles of Geotechnical Engineering 8th Edition.pdf` (Das 8th ed., Ch. 17, §§17.1–17.3 and 17.9 (purposes of subsoil exploration, planning for soil exploration, boring methods, soil exploration report)), held in the local reference library — see
> [[Reference library]]. This note is a paraphrased working aid, not the
> document. Values, clauses and criteria must be read from the source before
> they are relied on, and engineering conclusions need qualified review.

**Outcome.** A borehole layout, depth schedule, sampling and in-situ testing plan matched to the structure and the ground, with the desk study and reconnaissance completed first.

## Inputs

- Structure type, footprint, column and wall loads, and future use
- Available published information: geological maps, soil survey maps, previous investigation reports for nearby sites
- Site reconnaissance observations: topography, vegetation, drainage, open cuts, cracking in nearby structures
- Regulatory and contractual requirements for investigation scope

## Procedure

1. Phase 1 — compile structural information: loads, footprint, span lengths for bridges, and the requirements of the applicable code.
2. Phase 2 — collect existing subsurface information from geological and soil survey mapping and from previous investigations for adjacent works; this often removes a large part of the guesswork and reduces the field programme.
3. Phase 3 — walk the site: note vegetation types that indicate soil conditions, accessibility, drainage into and out of the site, exposures in open cuts, and distress in nearby structures that may indicate soft or reactive ground.
4. Phase 4 — set out the boreholes. Start with a boring at each corner of the structure and one at the centre, then add holes where the ground proves variable. Use the source's tabulated indicative spacings by project type (buildings, highways, earth dams, subdivisions) as a starting point.
5. Set borehole depths so they pass through unsuitable material into firm bearing strata. For buildings, use the ASCE-style rule: compute the depth at which the net stress increase from the structure falls to about a tenth of the average applied pressure, and the depth at which it falls to about five percent of the effective overburden stress; take the shallower of those two, unless bedrock is met first. The empirical rules based on number of storeys in the source give a quick first estimate.
6. Schedule sampling and in-situ testing at intervals through each hole (commonly at regular vertical spacing and at every change of stratum), specifying disturbed versus undisturbed sampling by the laboratory tests required.
7. Include groundwater observation: record water levels during and after drilling and install standpipes or piezometers where pore pressures matter.
8. Plan for rock coring where bedrock is expected, and specify core size and recovery requirements.
9. Specify the reporting requirements: logs, laboratory schedule, groundwater records, and the interpreted ground model.

## Decision rules

- Extend at least some boreholes well below the depth of influence — a compressible layer below the investigated depth is the classic cause of unexpected settlement.
- Where bedrock is met at shallow depth, prove it is bedrock and not a boulder or a cemented band by coring a minimum penetration into it.
- Investigation spacing must respond to the observed variability: uniform ground justifies wider spacing, variable ground demands more holes, not more laboratory tests on the same samples.
- Undisturbed samples are required for consolidation, triaxial and unconfined compression testing; disturbed samples suffice for classification, grading and Atterberg limits.
- Always record groundwater; a single reading at the end of drilling in a low-permeability soil is not a groundwater level.

## Checks

- Check the planned depths against the calculated depth of stress influence, not just a rule of thumb.
- Check the programme covers all structures on the site, including retaining walls, pavements and services, not only the main building.
- Check that the investigation will produce the specific parameters the design method needs — plan backwards from the design calculations.

## Common errors

- Terminating boreholes at a fixed depth regardless of the stress influence.
- Skipping the desk study and reconnaissance and going straight to drilling.
- Failing to install groundwater monitoring, so pore pressure assumptions cannot be validated.

## Caveats

- Textbook learning material, not a design standard — verify method currency and all values against the source and Australian practice before use
- Borehole spacing and depth tables in the source are indicative only.
- Australian site investigation practice is governed by AS 1726 and, for road and rail projects, by authority-specific requirements.
